About NITIZEN

Built for the Citizen Nigeria Deserves

NITIZEN fuses "Nigeria" with "citizen" — a deliberate declaration that active, informed citizenship is the foundation of the country we want to build.

Nigeria is at a crossroads. With a median age of under 20, its population is overwhelmingly youthful — yet civic participation, policy literacy, and democratic engagement remain dangerously low. NITIZEN is built to change that.

We are not a passive audience to governance; we are its architects, its critics, its conscience. Through AI-assisted civic learning, editorial analysis, civic reporting, institutional tracking, and creative community engagement — NITIZEN creates a digital environment where citizens don't just consume information. They act on it.

Our immediate focus is the 2027 general elections: preparing citizens not merely to vote, but to vote with clarity, purpose, and accountability. But the mission extends far beyond any single cycle — NITIZEN is long-term civic infrastructure for a better Nigeria.

🎯 Our Mission

To build a technology-powered civic education platform equipping Nigerians — particularly youth — with the knowledge, critical thinking tools, and participatory channels needed for active democratic engagement.

🌍 Our Vision

A Nigeria where every citizen — regardless of class, geography, or education — can meaningfully engage with political power, hold leadership accountable, and shape national development decisions.

⚙️ Our Approach

We leverage technology, media, and storytelling as strategic tools — combining AI, multimedia criticism, and long-form civic writing into a single platform accessible to every Nigerian with a smartphone.

📍 Our Context

Operating at the intersection of Policy & Advocacy, Electoral Engagement, Accountability & Transparency, and Social Inclusion — the thematic areas defining good governance for Nigeria's present and future.

Our Scale

Reaching Every Nigerian Corner

NITIZEN is designed for scale — from urban Lagos to rural Yobe, every Nigerian with a smartphone is our audience. The 2027 elections are the first major milestone. The mission is generational.

Governance · Economy · No. 001

The Promise and the Price: What Tinubu's Subsidy Removal Means for the Nigerian Poor

NITIZEN EDITORIAL · MAY 2026 · 8 MIN READ

When President Bola Tinubu stepped onto the podium at Eagle Square on May 29, 2023, and declared that "fuel subsidy is gone," the crowd erupted. For many Nigerians — particularly the elite commentariat and economic reform advocates — it was the sound of a long-overdue reckoning. The subsidy had for years consumed hundreds of billions of naira annually — resources that could have funded hospitals, built roads, trained teachers, or shored up the naira. Economists had warned of its unsustainability. The IMF had recommended its removal.

But economic arguments, however valid, do not exist in a vacuum. They exist in the real conditions of real people — and the real conditions of Nigeria are these: over 100 million citizens living below the poverty line; a minimum wage that had not been meaningfully reviewed in years; a naira in freefall; and an inflation rate that peaked at 34.8% in December 2023 — the highest level recorded in nearly three decades.

"When you remove a subsidy in a country with no social safety net, no functioning public transportation, and a collapsed manufacturing sector, you are not reforming an economy. You are conducting an experiment on the poor."

The Arithmetic of Suffering

Before May 29, 2023, Nigerians purchased petrol at approximately ₦185 per litre. Within weeks, the pump price had climbed to over ₦600. By 2025, it crossed ₦1,000 in many parts of the country. For a bus driver in Aba, a petty trader in Maiduguri, a bricklayer in Kaduna — this was not a policy reform. It was an economic catastrophe visited upon them without warning, without cushion, and without a plan.

Transportation costs doubled, then tripled. Food prices spiked. The cost of running generators became unbearable for households already stretched to breaking point. Nigeria's inflation rate climbed to 34.8% at its peak. Real incomes collapsed. By the NBS's own multidimensional poverty estimates, over 60% of Nigerians were already poor before the removal. The subsidy shock deepened that poverty further.

The Missing Cushion

The Tinubu administration announced palliatives — a ₦8,000 monthly cash transfer to vulnerable Nigerians. The programme was delayed. Its coverage was incomplete. The verification mechanisms were inadequate. Millions who needed support never received it. Meanwhile, the naira collapsed from approximately ₦465 to the dollar in May 2023 to over ₦1,500 by early 2024.

The moral case against the subsidy removal as executed is not that the reform was wrong. It is that the protection that should have accompanied structural adjustment was absent, inadequate, or delayed. Reform that inflicts pain without providing protection is not bold governance. It is indifference dressed in the language of fiscal prudence.

The Question of Accountability

Nigeria's subsidy expenditure was for years a black box — a source of endemic corruption, fuel importation cartels, and opaque transfer mechanisms. The savings that were supposed to flow from its removal have not been transparently tracked. Citizens deserve to know where the billions that were previously consumed by the subsidy are now going, and whether they are reaching the infrastructure and social programmes they were promised for. Conscience Letters will continue to track this.

This piece represents the editorial position of NITIZEN's Conscience Letters initiative. We welcome responses at hello@nitizen.ng.

Electoral Democracy · No. 002

INEC and the Integrity Question: Can We Trust the 2027 Electoral Process?

NITIZEN EDITORIAL · MAY 2026 · 6 MIN READ

The Independent National Electoral Commission carries a name that, for many Nigerians, has become ironic. "Independent" — from what, exactly? The INEC that conducted the 2023 general elections oversaw a process that generated more litigation, more controversy, and more citizen outrage than arguably any election in Nigeria's Fourth Republic history. The Supreme Court eventually upheld the presidential result. But a court's verdict on the legality of a process is not the same as a citizenry's confidence in its integrity.

"An election that citizens do not trust is not a democracy functioning. It is a democracy performing."

What Went Wrong in 2023

The 2023 presidential election was, by INEC's own design, supposed to be its most transparent ever. The Bimodal Voter Accreditation System (BVAS) was introduced to prevent the manipulation of voter accreditation. The INEC Result Viewing Portal (IReV) was launched with the promise of real-time upload of polling unit results.

Both innovations failed, or were failed, at critical moments. The IReV portal experienced what INEC described as technical difficulties during the critical early hours of result transmission. Many results appeared hours or days late. Discrepancies between polling unit results and collated figures were documented by domestic and international observers — including the EU EOM and the Carter Center.

The Road to 2027

What must change before 2027 to restore citizen confidence? Three things, at minimum: transparent technology, independent oversight, and civic preparedness. On technology: INEC must demonstrate, through pre-election public testing, that result management systems are tamper-resistant. On oversight: the appointment process of INEC's leadership must be restructured to insulate the commission from executive pressure. On civic preparedness: NITIZEN exists to address this third dimension — a citizenry that understands the electoral process is harder to disenfranchise.

The 2027 elections are Nigeria's next defining democratic moment. Whether they represent progress or regression will depend significantly on what INEC, the political class, and citizens do between now and then.

Legislature · Accountability · No. 003

The Legislature We Deserve: On Nigeria's National Assembly Budget and Its Accountability Deficit

NITIZEN EDITORIAL · MAY 2026 · 5 MIN READ

In a country where a public school teacher earns approximately ₦30,000 a month — often paid late, sometimes not at all — the Nigerian National Assembly received a statutory transfer of ₦344.85 billion in the 2025 Appropriation Act. Beyond this, BudgIT documented that lawmakers inserted 11,122 additional projects worth ₦6.93 trillion into the 2025 budget — a figure that dwarfs the statutory allocation itself.

"A parliament that costs more than it produces — in legislation, in oversight, in service — is not a parliament. It is a welfare scheme for the politically connected."

The Oversight Paradox

The National Assembly's constitutional role is threefold: legislation, representation, and oversight. Of these three, it is in oversight that the legislature's failure is most consequential and most visible. The executive has, in successive administrations, expanded its reach — spending without appropriation, deploying security forces without legislative authorisation, managing state resources with opacity that would be challenged in any functioning democracy. Nigeria's National Assembly, with rare exceptions, has watched this happen.

What a Legislature Should Cost — and Deliver

The question is not whether a legislature requires adequate funding. It does. The question is proportionality: whether the cost of the institution bears any reasonable relationship to what it delivers for the citizens who fund it. By that measure, Nigeria's National Assembly is not delivering. The ratio of legislative output to legislative expenditure is deeply unfavourable.

And until Nigerian citizens begin to demand otherwise — to track legislative performance, to know how their representatives vote, to show up at constituency offices and demand accountability — the legislature will continue to be exactly as expensive and exactly as unproductive as it has always been. This is what Conscience Letters is for.

Extrajudicial Killing of Mene Ogidi, 28 — April 26, 2026 · Case #RF-001-2026

Mene Ogidi, a 28-year-old Nigerian, was shot at close range by ASP Nuhu Usman of the Nigeria Police Force at the Benin Motor Park along the Warri–Sapele Expressway, Effurun, Delta State. A widely circulated video showed Ogidi seated on the ground with his hands tied behind his back before being shot by an officer in plain clothes. He had left home to collect a waybill on behalf of a friend in Sapele and never returned alive. His family disclosed that police had previously killed his younger brother in a separate incident. The Force Disciplinary Committee found ASP Usman acted "in gross violation of Force Order 237 and other extant regulations governing the use of firearms." Inspector-General of Police Tunji Disu approved Usman's immediate dismissal. Delta State Governor Sheriff Oborevwori described the killing as "unwarranted, unprovoked and barbaric." Criminal prosecution proceedings are underway.

🏛️ Bad Governance · Lagos State
Documented
Waterfront Community Demolitions Without Statutory Compensation · Case #RF-003-2026

Between 2023 and 2025, Lagos State government carried out demolitions of waterfront communities with affected residents reporting absence of prior notice, resettlement alternatives, or compensation as required under the Lagos State Urban and Regional Planning and Development Law. Human rights organisations documented the displacements. See-gnal calls on Lagos State to publish a complete resettlement and compensation record.

Why Your Support Matters

Independent Civic Journalism Costs Something

Nigeria has no shortage of media. It has a shortage of civic media — journalism that is not funded by government contracts, not shaped by advertiser interests, and not owned by political actors with stakes in what gets reported.

Conscience Letters is built on a different model: funded by readers who believe that accountability journalism is a public good. Every reader who becomes a Supporter makes it possible to commission a piece, pay a writer fairly, and publish something that would otherwise never get written.

  • ✦No government advertising — our editorial line answers to readers, not ministries
  • ✦No sponsored content — every Conscience Letters piece is editorially independent
  • ✦Writers are paid — your support goes directly to compensating civic writers fairly
  • ✦Full financial transparency — annual supporter report published showing income and use of funds

Privacy Policy

NITIZEN's full data privacy policy — how we collect, use, store, and protect your personal data in compliance with the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 and NDPC regulations.

Summary: NITIZEN collects only data you voluntarily submit through our forms. We do not sell your data, track you, or share your information with third parties for commercial purposes. AskNaija does not store your questions. You have full rights to access, correct, and delete your data at any time. We are regulated under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023.

1. Who We Are

NITIZEN is a civic intelligence platform operated by Ekene Godseffort Chimarin (Lead Campaigner, Founder). Contact: hello@nitizen.ng. NITIZEN operates from Nigeria. For data protection purposes, NITIZEN is the Data Controller. Formspree Inc. acts as a Data Processor on our behalf for form submissions.

2. Data We Collect

When you use any form on NITIZEN, we collect: Name (for correspondence), Email address (to respond), Phone number (optional, where requested), Message/submission content, and Location/state where provided. AskNaija is entirely client-side. Your questions are processed locally in your browser. No question you type is transmitted to any server, stored, or logged. We do not use cookies for tracking or advertising. We do not use Google Analytics or any third-party analytics trackers.

3. Your Rights Under the NDPA 2023

You have the right to: access your data, correct inaccuracies, request deletion, withdraw consent, and data portability. Exercise any right by emailing hello@nitizen.ng — subject: "Data Rights Request". We respond within 7 working days.

4. Data Retention

Contact and editorial submissions: 12 months from submission date. Published submissions (RedFlag, OpenMic, Critika, Ledger): retained as part of the public civic record — you may request removal. Membership records: 7 years. AskNaija interactions: zero retention.

5. Third Parties

Formspree Inc. processes form submissions on our behalf. Netlify Inc. hosts the NITIZEN website. We have no other third-party data processors. No advertising networks. No social media trackers.

6. Complaints

Contact us directly: hello@nitizen.ng. Or file a complaint with the Nigeria Data Protection Commission: ndpc.gov.ng.
