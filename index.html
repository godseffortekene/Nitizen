About NITIZEN

Built for the Citizen Nigeria Deserves

NITIZEN fuses "Nigeria" with "citizen" — a deliberate declaration that active, informed citizenship is the foundation of the country we want to build.

Nigeria is at a crossroads. With a median age of under 20, its population is overwhelmingly youthful — yet civic participation, policy literacy, and democratic engagement remain dangerously low. NITIZEN is built to change that.

We are not a passive audience to governance; we are its architects, its critics, its conscience. Through AI-assisted civic learning, editorial analysis, civic reporting, institutional tracking, and creative community engagement across three core initiatives — with See-gnal housing four dedicated civic action channels, NITIZEN creates a digital environment where citizens don't just consume information — they act on it.

Our immediate focus is the 2027 general elections: preparing citizens not merely to vote, but to vote with clarity, purpose, and accountability. But the mission extends far beyond any single cycle — NITIZEN is long-term civic infrastructure for a better Nigeria.

🎯 Our Mission

To build a technology-powered civic education platform equipping Nigerians — particularly youth — with the knowledge, critical thinking tools, and participatory channels needed for active democratic engagement.

🌍 Our Vision

A Nigeria where every citizen — regardless of class, geography, or education — can meaningfully engage with political power, hold leadership accountable, and shape national development decisions.

⚙️ Our Approach

We leverage technology, media, and storytelling as strategic tools — combining AI, multimedia criticism, and long-form civic writing into a single platform accessible to every Nigerian with a smartphone.

📍 Our Context

Operating at the intersection of Policy & Advocacy, Electoral Engagement, Accountability & Transparency, and Social Inclusion — the thematic areas defining good governance for Nigeria's present and future.

The Civic Gap We Are Closing

Nigeria faces a deepening civic participation crisis. Despite being home to over 100 million young people, youth engagement with governance structures remains critically low. Voter apathy, policy illiteracy, and disengagement from democratic processes are widespread.

Extrajudicial Killing of Mene Ogidi, 28 — April 26, 2026 · Case #RF-001-2026

Mene Ogidi, a 28-year-old Nigerian, was shot at close range by ASP Nuhu Usman of the Nigeria Police Force at the Benin Motor Park along the Warri–Sapele Expressway, Effurun, Delta State. A widely circulated video showed Ogidi seated on the ground with his hands tied behind his back before being shot by an officer in plain clothes. He had left home to collect a waybill on behalf of a friend in Sapele and never returned alive. The Force Disciplinary Committee found ASP Usman acted "in gross violation of Force Order 237 and other extant regulations governing the use of firearms." Inspector-General of Police Tunji Disu approved Usman's immediate dismissal. Criminal prosecution proceedings are underway.

The Promise and the Price: What Tinubu's Subsidy Removal Means for the Nigerian Poor

Conscience Letters No. 001 · May 2026 · 8 min read

When President Bola Tinubu declared at his inauguration that "fuel subsidy is gone," the crowd cheered. Eighteen months later, the country is still counting the cost — and the poor are paying the highest price. This is not merely an economic argument. It is a moral one.

The removal of Nigeria's fuel subsidy was, by most conventional economic analysis, long overdue. The subsidy had for years consumed hundreds of billions of naira annually — resources that could have funded hospitals, built roads, trained teachers, or shored up the naira. Economists had warned of its unsustainability. The IMF had recommended its removal. The consensus among technocrats was clear: the subsidy was a leaky bucket that disproportionately benefited the wealthy, who consume more fuel and own more cars.

But the question this essay asks is not whether the subsidy should have been removed. It is: removed how, at what pace, with what protection for those who could not absorb the shock?

The Human Arithmetic

For a Nigerian earning the federal minimum wage of ₦30,000 monthly — itself a figure that had not been revised since 2019 at the time of the removal — the doubling, then tripling of petrol prices was not an inconvenience. It was a collapse. Transport costs rose immediately. Goods that moved by road rose with them. Food prices spiked. The market women, the keke operators, the traders who depended on diesel-powered generators to keep their wares cold or their workshops lit — they absorbed costs they had no capacity to absorb.

Nigeria's inflation rate climbed to a peak of 34.8% in December 2023 — levels not seen in nearly three decades. Real incomes collapsed. By the NBS's own multidimensional poverty estimates, over 60% of Nigerians were already poor before the removal. The subsidy shock deepened that poverty, compressed that space, pushed millions further from the margin.

The Missing Cushion

The Tinubu administration announced palliatives — a ₦8,000 monthly cash transfer to vulnerable Nigerians, distributed via the National Social Register. The programme was delayed. Its coverage was incomplete. The verification mechanisms were inadequate. Millions who needed support never received it. Meanwhile, the naira was further liberalised — collapsing from approximately ₦465 to the dollar in May 2023 to over ₦1,500 by early 2024.

The moral case against the subsidy removal as executed is not that the reform was wrong. It is that the protection that should have accompanied structural adjustment was absent, inadequate, or delayed. Reform that inflicts pain without providing protection is not bold governance. It is indifference dressed in the language of fiscal prudence.

The Question of Accountability

Nigeria's subsidy expenditure was for years a black box — a source of endemic corruption, fuel importation cartels, and opaque transfer mechanisms. The savings that were supposed to flow from its removal have not been transparently tracked. Citizens deserve to know where the billions that were previously consumed by the subsidy are now going, and whether they are reaching the infrastructure and social programmes they were promised for.

Conscience Letters will continue to track this. The price of a litre of petrol is now public data. The whereabouts of the fiscal savings should be too.

INEC and the Integrity Question: Can We Trust the 2027 Electoral Process?

Conscience Letters No. 002 · May 2026 · 6 min read

The Independent National Electoral Commission has a credibility problem. From the results sheets of 2023 to the electronic transmission debates of 2019, Nigeria's electoral umpire carries the weight of unresolved doubts. With 2027 approaching, citizens deserve a frank assessment.

The February and March 2023 elections produced multiple court challenges, a presidential tribunal, and — eventually — a Supreme Court ruling that upheld Tinubu's victory while acknowledging documented irregularities in the conduct of the election. The BVAS machines introduced to improve result collation integrity performed unevenly across polling units. The iREV portal — where results were to be uploaded in real time — suffered functionality issues on election day at critical moments.

These are not partisan claims. They are the findings of domestic and international observer missions, including the EU EOM and the Carter Center, which documented specific, verifiable failures in electoral administration. The question for 2027 is not whether these failures occurred — they are on record — but whether the institutional lessons have been genuinely internalised.

What Trust Requires

Electoral trust in Nigeria requires not merely the absence of fraud but the presence of mechanisms that make fraud difficult, detectable, and prosecutable. It requires transparent voter registration, functioning result management systems, independent INEC financing that insulates the commission from executive pressure, and a judiciary that can adjudicate electoral disputes with speed and impartiality.

Citizens must now demand these mechanisms publicly, consistently, and loudly — because democratic reform in Nigeria has historically happened only when citizens held the pressure. Conscience Letters will track INEC's institutional reforms and preparedness as 2027 approaches. The future of democracy here is not decided on election day. It is decided in the months and years before.

Legislature · Accountability

The Legislature We Deserve: On Nigeria's National Assembly Budget and Its Accountability Deficit

Conscience Letters No. 003 · May 2026 · 5 min read

Nigeria's National Assembly received over ₦344 billion in statutory transfers in 2025 — while BudgIT documented lawmakers inserting ₦6.93 trillion in additional projects into the budget. What does this tell us about the legislature's relationship with the people it claims to serve?

The National Assembly is constitutionally charged with the power of appropriation — the right to pass, adjust, and approve the federal budget. This is one of the legislature's most important democratic functions: the power to allocate public resources in the public interest. It is also, in Nigeria, one of the most abused.

The phenomenon of "budget padding" — the insertion of constituency projects and line items by individual lawmakers — is not unique to Nigeria. What is uniquely troubling here is the scale, the opacity, and the absence of meaningful public accountability for how these funds are eventually deployed. BudgIT's tracking of constituency projects has repeatedly documented projects listed as completed that were never begun, and funds disbursed for infrastructure that cannot be located on the ground.

The Cost of an Unaccountable Legislature

A legislature that appropriates nearly ₦344 billion for its own operations while overseeing the insertion of trillions in projects with poor completion rates is not merely wasteful. It is structurally extractive — designed to harvest public resources rather than deploy them toward public ends.

The remedy is civic pressure, applied persistently: demanding constituency project audits, insisting on published procurement records for NASS projects, and — most critically — building a culture in which lawmakers know that their constituents are watching, that the data is being compiled, and that accountability will eventually arrive. Conscience Letters exists, in part, to begin that accounting.
