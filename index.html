About NITIZEN

Built for the Citizen Nigeria Deserves

NITIZEN fuses “Nigeria” with “citizen” — a deliberate declaration that active, informed citizenship is the foundation of the country we want to build.

Nigeria is at a crossroads. With a median age of under 20, its population is overwhelmingly youthful — yet civic participation, policy literacy, and democratic engagement remain dangerously low. NITIZEN is built to change that.

We are not a passive audience to governance; we are its architects, its critics, its conscience. Through AI-assisted civic learning, editorial analysis, civic reporting, institutional tracking, and creative community engagement — NITIZEN creates a digital environment where citizens don’t just consume information. They act on it.

Our immediate focus is the 2027 general elections: preparing citizens not merely to vote, but to vote with clarity, purpose, and accountability. But the mission extends far beyond any single cycle — NITIZEN is long-term civic infrastructure for a better Nigeria.

The Promise and the Price: What Tinubu's Subsidy Removal Means for the Nigerian Poor

NITIZEN EDITORIAL · MAY 2026 · 8 MIN READ

When President Bola Tinubu stepped onto the podium at Eagle Square on May 29, 2023, and declared that "fuel subsidy is gone," the crowd erupted. For many Nigerians — particularly the elite commentariat and economic reform advocates — it was the sound of a long-overdue reckoning. The subsidy, they argued, had for decades been a drain on the public treasury: a leaking pipe that cost the government trillions of naira annually while disproportionately benefiting the wealthy who owned multiple cars and the fuel importers who had turned the system into a private ATM.

They were not entirely wrong. The fuel subsidy regime was ridden with corruption, opacity, and inefficiency. The NNPCL's books were a maze. The subsidy verification processes were susceptible to manipulation. Billions disappeared into a system that was, at its worst, a sophisticated mechanism for transferring public wealth into private hands.

But economic arguments, however valid, do not exist in a vacuum. They exist in the real conditions of real people — and the real conditions of Nigeria are these: over 100 million citizens living below the poverty line[¹]; a minimum wage that had not been meaningfully reviewed in years; a naira in freefall; and an inflation rate that climbed to 28.92% by December 2023[²] — and would continue rising to a peak of 34.8% by February 2024 — the highest levels recorded since Nigeria began tracking CPI inflation in its current form.

"When you remove a subsidy in a country with no social safety net, no functioning public transportation, and a collapsed manufacturing sector, you are not reforming an economy. You are conducting an experiment on the poor."

The Arithmetic of Suffering

Let us be precise about what happened. Before May 29, 2023, Nigerians purchased petrol at approximately ₦185 per litre.[³] Verified Within weeks of the subsidy removal, the pump price had climbed to over ₦600 per litre. Verified By 2025, it crossed ₦1,000 in many parts of the country. For a bus driver in Aba, a petty trader in Maiduguri, a bricklayer in Kaduna — this was not a policy reform. It was an economic catastrophe visited upon them without warning, without cushion, and without a plan.

Transportation costs doubled, then tripled. The price of food — overwhelmingly transported by road across Nigeria's vast geography — rose in lockstep. The cost of running generators, which millions of Nigerians depend on because the national grid delivers on average fewer than eight hours of electricity daily[⁴], became unbearable for households already stretched to breaking point.

The government promised palliatives. They came late, were poorly targeted, and were widely regarded as inadequate. A one-time payment of ₦8,000 to the poorest Nigerians[⁵] Verified — the figure that circulated in early government communications — was widely mocked. For context: ₦8,000 would not cover a week's transportation costs for a Lagos market woman at the new petrol prices.

The Moral Question

This piece is not an argument for the return of the fuel subsidy. It is an argument about how reform is conducted — and who bears its cost. Economic reform that places its entire burden on those least equipped to absorb it is not courage. It is negligence dressed in the language of progress.

The Tinubu administration had options. A phased removal could have given households and businesses time to adjust. Direct and verifiable cash transfers to the most vulnerable — managed transparently — could have cushioned the blow. Investment in public transportation infrastructure, in parallel with subsidy removal, would have created an alternative to private vehicle dependence. None of these were in place when the subsidy was yanked away on inauguration day.

The price of that unpreparedness has been paid — is being paid — by the Nigerian poor. This is the record. And a democracy worthy of the name demands that we read it clearly.

Electoral Democracy · Conscience Letters No. 002

The Ballot and the Broken Promise: Nigeria's Electoral History and What 2027 Must Learn From It

NITIZEN EDITORIAL · MAY 2026 · 12 MIN READ

The Independent National Electoral Commission carries a name that, for many Nigerians, has become ironic. "Independent" — from what, exactly? The INEC that conducted the 2023 general elections oversaw a process that generated more litigation, more controversy, and more citizen outrage than arguably any election in Nigeria's Fourth Republic history. The Supreme Court eventually upheld the presidential result.[¹] Verified But a court's verdict on the legality of a process is not the same as a citizenry's confidence in its integrity.

As the country turns its attention toward 2027, the integrity of the electoral process is not merely a procedural question. It is the central question of Nigerian democracy. An election that citizens do not trust — regardless of its technical outcomes — corrodes the legitimacy of governance and deepens the disengagement that already plagues the republic.

"An election that citizens do not trust is not a democracy functioning. It is a democracy performing."

But to understand where we are, we must understand where we have been. Nigeria's electoral history is not a story of steady progress. It is a story of cycles — of promise, manipulation, crisis, and partial recovery — repeated with enough variation to discourage hope and enough resilience to refuse extinction.

The First Republic and the Original Sin (1959–1966)

Nigeria's first federal election under universal suffrage, held in December 1959, was in many ways a colonial handover exercise. The three dominant parties — the NPC in the north, the NCNC in the east, and the Action Group in the west — contested along lines that mirrored the country's regional and ethnic divisions far more than any coherent ideological differentiation. The NPC, dominant in the more populous north, emerged with the most seats and formed a coalition government with the NCNC under Sir Abubakar Tafawa Balewa as Prime Minister.[²]

What followed was the foundational lesson Nigeria has never fully absorbed: that electoral competition in a multi-ethnic, multi-religious polity without strong civic institutions and independent adjudication is a recipe not for democratic consolidation but for zero-sum warfare. The 1964–65 federal and regional elections descended into violence, rigging, and boycotts of a scale that shocked even seasoned colonial administrators. The Western Region crisis of 1965 — in which election results were so brazenly manipulated that rival politicians declared parallel victories — set the conditions for the first military coup of January 15, 1966.[²] Nigeria's first experiment with democracy lasted six years. It was killed not by an external enemy but by the failure of its own political class to respect the verdict of the ballot.

Military Interruptions and Managed Transitions (1966–1999)

The thirty-three years between the first coup and the return to civilian rule in 1999 were not a democratic void — they were a democratic graveyard in which civilian institutions were repeatedly interred just as they began to take root. General Yakubu Gowon's regime promised transition and delivered civil war. Murtala Muhammed's brief tenure ended in assassination. Olusegun Obasanjo's first administration oversaw the transition to the Second Republic in 1979 — genuinely the most credible electoral transfer of power Nigeria had seen — only for that republic to be terminated by Muhammadu Buhari's coup in December 1983, citing the same election rigging that the Second Republic's political class had perfected.[³]

Ibrahim Babangida's eight-year rule produced the most consequential election in Nigerian history: the June 12, 1993 presidential election, won decisively by Chief MKO Abiola of the Social Democratic Party. The annulment of that election by Babangida — widely understood to be the fairest Nigeria had ever conducted — remains the deepest wound in the country's democratic memory.[³] June 12 taught Nigeria two things: that Nigerians could, when given genuine options and free conditions, vote across ethnic lines; and that the military would not accept results that threatened its institutional interests. The protests, the political crisis, and the eventual death of Abiola in detention in 1998 marked the moral nadir of Nigerian governance. The June 12 wound has never fully healed — and it should not, because its lesson is still being learned.

General Sani Abacha's subsequent rule (1993–1998) was a dictatorship with no serious democratic pretension. His death in June 1998 opened the path for Abdulsalami Abubakar's compressed but comparatively credible transition, which produced the election of Olusegun Obasanjo as president in February 1999.[³]

The Fourth Republic: Patterns of Partial Democracy (1999–2015)

The elections of 1999 returned Nigeria to civilian rule, but they did not return it to democratic integrity. The 2003 elections, conducted by INEC under Justice Abel Guobadia, were widely criticised for rigging, violence, and result fabrication on a scale that dwarfed anything in the post-1999 period.[⁴] Obasanjo's reelection was secured through a process that domestic observers, the EU Election Observation Mission, and civil society groups described in unambiguous terms. "Massaged results" was the diplomatic phrasing; "stolen" was the more honest one.

The 2007 elections — transitioning power to Umaru Musa Yar'Adua — were arguably worse. INEC under Maurice Iwu presided over a process that the EU observation mission described as falling "far short of basic international and regional standards."[⁴] Yar'Adua himself, in his inauguration speech, acknowledged the "imperfections" of the election that brought him to power — a remarkable admission that became a statement of intent when his administration initiated the 2008 Electoral Reform Committee under Justice Muhammadu Uwais.

The Uwais Report recommendations — many of which were subsequently diluted or ignored by the National Assembly — would have fundamentally restructured Nigeria's electoral architecture. What emerged instead was a partial reform: enough to make 2011 significantly more credible, but not enough to close the structural gaps that enable manipulation. The 2011 elections under Attahiru Jega delivered the most widely accepted result since 1993. They also produced post-election violence in twelve northern states that killed over 800 people[⁴] — a reminder that democratic credibility and political stability are not the same thing in a polity as complex as Nigeria.

2015: The Pivot That Was and Wasn't

The 2015 general elections were, by measurable standards, a genuine turning point. For the first time in Nigerian history, an incumbent president — Goodluck Jonathan — conceded defeat and congratulated his opponent before the formal announcement of results.[⁵] Muhammadu Buhari's victory under the All Progressives Congress marked the first democratic transfer of power between parties in Nigeria's history. The international community celebrated. Nigerians — many of them — wept with relief. It felt, for a moment, like the June 12 wound might finally heal.

The moment deserved that optimism. What followed tested it. The 2019 elections, also conducted under Buhari, were marred by voter suppression, violence — particularly in Rivers State — and widespread allegations of result manipulation. INEC's credibility, rebuilt under Jega, began to erode again under Mahmood Yakubu. The passage of the Electoral Act 2022 — which introduced the provision for electronic transmission of results and the use of BVAS — represented genuine legislative progress.[⁵] But legislation is only as strong as the institution mandated to implement it.

What Went Wrong in 2023

The 2023 presidential election was, by INEC's own design, supposed to be its most transparent ever. The Bimodal Voter Accreditation System (BVAS) was introduced to prevent the manipulation of voter accreditation.[⁶] The INEC Result Viewing Portal (IReV) was launched with the promise of real-time upload of polling unit results — a measure designed to make result falsification visible and traceable.

Both innovations failed, or were failed, at critical moments. The IReV portal experienced what INEC described as technical difficulties during the critical early hours of result transmission. Many results appeared hours or days late. Discrepancies between polling unit results and collated figures were documented by domestic and international observers. INEC's own officials were arraigned in some states for alleged electoral malpractice. Verified[⁷]

The three-way contest between Tinubu (APC), Atiku Abubakar (PDP), and Peter Obi (Labour Party) added a dimension Nigeria had not seen in the Fourth Republic: a genuinely competitive third-party candidate drawing massive youth support, the so-called "Obidient" movement, which demonstrated that Nigerian voters — particularly younger ones — were capable of refusing the binary between established parties. Obi's 25.7% of the presidential vote represented the strongest third-party performance since 1979. The movement's energy was real. Its structural limitations — lack of ground infrastructure, party machinery, and legislative presence — were equally real.

The Supreme Court upheld Tinubu's election on February 6, 2024.[¹] The ruling resolved the legal question. It did not resolve the credibility question. And in a democracy, both matter — because one is about the law, and the other is about the people who live under it.

The Lessons History Has Already Taught Us

Six decades of electoral experience have produced a set of lessons that Nigeria's political class has studied carefully — and applied, in many cases, for the purpose of subverting rather than strengthening democracy. For citizens, those same lessons are instruction manuals for civic vigilance:

Lesson One: Ethnic and regional arithmetic does not determine — but does constrain — electoral outcomes. From 1959 to 2023, the dominant pattern has been coalition-building along ethnic and regional lines. But 1993 and 2015 demonstrated that when civic conditions allow genuine choice, Nigerians vote across those lines. The pattern is not destiny. It is a habit — and habits can be broken by a sufficiently motivated citizenry.

Lesson Two: Institutional independence is everything. Every credible election Nigeria has conducted — 1979, 1993, 2011, 2015 — was overseen by an electoral commission that had, or was perceived to have, some degree of genuine independence from incumbent power. Every deeply discredited election involved an INEC (or its predecessors) compromised by executive influence, inadequate resourcing, or institutional cowardice. The appointment process, funding independence, and security of tenure of electoral commissioners are not administrative details. They are the architecture of democratic legitimacy.

Lesson Three: Technology is a tool, not a solution. BVAS and IReV in 2023 were theoretically sound innovations. They were not sufficiently protected from institutional failure, network unreliability, or the human decisions made at collation centres. Electronic systems amplify integrity when the surrounding institution has integrity. When that condition is absent, they amplify the appearance of transparency while enabling the reality of manipulation.

Lesson Four: Civic participation determines the cost of rigging. In every well-documented instance of electoral fraud in Nigeria, the fraud has been easier where voter apathy was highest. Large turnouts, active polling unit monitors, and a citizenry that knows its rights and acts on them raise the cost of manipulation to the point where it becomes operationally difficult. This is not idealism. It is the documented mechanics of election security.

Lesson Five: The absence of a PVC is a vote for whoever wins. Nigeria's voter registration is not automatic. It requires deliberate action. In 2023, an estimated 9.4 million registered voters did not collect their Permanent Voter Cards.[⁸] Those voters were registered in the system but effectively disenfranchised — not by anyone else's action, but by their own inaction. In a country where elections have been decided by margins of hundreds of thousands of votes, uncollected PVCs are not a bureaucratic oversight. They are an electoral catastrophe.

The Road to 2027: What Must Change

What must change before 2027 to restore citizen confidence? Three things, at minimum: transparent technology, independent oversight, and civic preparedness.

On technology: INEC must not only deploy electronic systems but must demonstrate, through pre-election public testing and open-source verification, that those systems are tamper-resistant and reliably connected. The failure of IReV in 2023 cannot be excused a second time. As of May 2026, INEC has signalled plans for a new voter register update exercise and improvements to its results transmission infrastructure. Citizens must demand proof, not promises.

On oversight: The composition of INEC's leadership and its appointment process must be revisited. An electoral commission whose chair is appointed solely by the executive, without meaningful legislative or civil society oversight, cannot credibly claim independence. Electoral reform legislation that restructures this dynamic is not a luxury — it is the minimum requirement for a credible 2027 election. Civil society organisations, including the Transition Monitoring Group, YIAGA Africa, and the Centre for Democracy and Development, have been consistent advocates for this reform. Their work deserves amplification and active citizen support.

On civic preparedness: this is where citizens hold power that no institution can take from them — if they choose to exercise it.

Where NITIZEN Stands in This Landscape

Nigeria has no shortage of election-related commentary. What it has a critical shortage of is structured, accessible, and permanently available civic intelligence — the kind that does not disappear after an election cycle, does not require a university degree to interpret, and does not serve the interest of any political party or commercial sponsor.

NITIZEN was built precisely for this gap. Conscience Letters provides the historical and analytical record — the institutional memory that prevents each election from being treated as if it exists in isolation from the sixty-year pattern that precedes it. AskNaija provides direct civic intelligence, answering specific questions about voter registration, polling procedures, and citizens' rights — not with opinion, but with sourced, verifiable information. See-gnal provides the architecture for citizen documentation — the RedFlag channel for reporting electoral irregularities in real time, The Ledger for tracking institutional performance, OpenMic for testimony, and Critika for the creative accountability that reaches audiences formal journalism cannot.

Together, these three initiatives form something Nigeria has not had before: a permanent, integrated civic infrastructure that is neither media nor government — but is accountable to both, because it is accountable to citizens. In the months leading to 2027, NITIZEN's role is not to tell Nigerians how to vote. It is to ensure that every Nigerian who chooses to vote is equipped with the knowledge, the tools, and the documentation capacity to make that vote count — and to defend it.

"The 2027 elections will not be decided by politicians alone. They will be decided by citizens who showed up — and by those who didn't."

Your Vote. Your PVC. Your Responsibility.

The 2027 general elections are constitutionally due in February 2027. INEC's Continuous Voter Registration (CVR) exercise provides rolling opportunities for new registrations and transfers. If you are not yet registered — or if you are registered but have not collected your Permanent Voter Card — the window is still open. But windows close.

Nigeria's history is littered with the consequences of citizen disengagement: elections decided by narrow margins while millions of eligible voters stayed home; mandates claimed by those who showed up while those with the most to lose calculated that their single vote would not matter. It will matter in 2027. It will matter in aggregate. And the aggregate is made up of individual decisions — yours, and your neighbour's, and your colleague's — made weeks or months before election day, when the quiet act of registering and collecting a card either happens or does not.

Do not outsource your democracy to those with more money and less conscience. Register. Collect your PVC. Know your polling unit. Show up.

2027 ELECTION READINESS — WHAT YOU MUST DO NOW

1. Register to vote — If you are 18 or older and not yet registered, visit the nearest INEC office or check inecnigeria.org for CVR dates and locations in your state.

2. Collect your PVC — Registration without collection is registration without power. Return to the INEC office where you registered and collect your Permanent Voter Card. Bring valid ID.

3. Confirm your polling unit — Use INEC's Voter Enrolment Details Verification System (VEDIS) at voters.inecnigeria.org to verify your registration status and confirm your assigned polling unit.

4. Mobilise your community — Every uncollected PVC in your household, your workplace, or your street is a vote that does not exist. Make this your personal civic project before 2027.

5. Stay informed — Return to NITIZEN for election-readiness guides, your rights on election day, how to report irregularities through See-gnal RedFlag, and AskNaija for any specific civic question you need answered.

The Legislature We Deserve: On Nigeria's National Assembly Budget and Its Accountability Deficit

NITIZEN EDITORIAL · MAY 2026 · 5 MIN READ

In a country where a public school teacher earns approximately ₦30,000 a month — often paid late, sometimes not at all — the Nigerian National Assembly received a statutory transfer of ₦344.85 billion in the 2025 Appropriation Act[¹] Verified, in addition to an extra ₦170 billion allocation captured under the Service Wide Vote[¹]. This covers the running costs of 469 legislators and their support apparatus: the staff, the vehicles, the security details, the overseas trips, the constituency allowances, and the considerable personal emoluments that make a Nigerian lawmaker one of the highest-paid in the world relative to per capita income. Beyond this, BudgIT documented that lawmakers inserted 11,122 additional projects worth ₦6.93 trillion into the 2025 budget[²] Verified — a figure that dwarfs the statutory allocation itself.

Let us sit with that ratio for a moment. Nigeria's GDP per capita is approximately $2,000[³] (World Bank, 2023). Its legislators earn, in allowances alone, multiples of what the average Nigerian earns in a lifetime. The legislature that is constitutionally mandated to represent the people has, over time, become an institution that is sustained by the people but oriented primarily toward itself.

"A parliament that costs more than it produces — in legislation, in oversight, in service — is not a parliament. It is a welfare scheme for the politically connected."

The Oversight Paradox

The National Assembly's constitutional role is threefold: legislation, representation, and oversight. Of these three, it is in oversight that the legislature's failure is most consequential and most visible. The executive has, in successive administrations, expanded its reach — spending without appropriation, deploying security forces without legislative authorisation, and managing state resources with opacity that would be challenged in any functioning democracy.

Nigeria's National Assembly, with rare exceptions, has watched this happen. It has held few meaningful investigative hearings that resulted in accountability. It has passed few consequential anti-corruption or institutional reform bills from its own initiative. Its relationship with the executive has too often resembled partnership rather than oversight — understandable given party dynamics, but constitutionally indefensible.

What a Legislature Should Cost — and Deliver

The question is not whether a legislature requires adequate funding. It does. Lawmaking, constituency service, and effective oversight require resources. The question is proportionality: whether the cost of the institution bears any reasonable relationship to what it delivers for the citizens who fund it.

By that measure, Nigeria's National Assembly is not delivering. The ratio of legislative output to legislative expenditure — measured not merely in bills passed but in laws that demonstrably improved Nigerian lives — is deeply unfavourable. And until Nigerian citizens begin to demand otherwise — to track legislative performance, to know how their representatives vote, to show up at constituency offices and demand accountability — the legislature will continue to be exactly as expensive and exactly as unproductive as it has always been.

This is what Conscience Letters is for. Not outrage — outrage is easy and cheap. Analysis is harder. And action — informed, sustained, civic action — is what actually changes things.

Electoral Democracy · Conscience Letters No. 004

INEC and the Integrity Question: Can We Trust the 2027 Electoral Process?

NITIZEN EDITORIAL · MAY 2026 · 6 MIN READ

The Independent National Electoral Commission carries a name that, for many Nigerians, has become ironic. “Independent” — from what, exactly? The INEC that conducted the 2023 general elections oversaw a process that generated more litigation, more controversy, and more citizen outrage than arguably any election in Nigeria’s Fourth Republic history. The Supreme Court eventually upheld the presidential result. Verified[¹] But a court’s verdict on the legality of a process is not the same as a citizenry’s confidence in its integrity.

As the country turns its attention toward 2027, the integrity of the electoral process is not merely a procedural question. It is the central question of Nigerian democracy. An election that citizens do not trust — regardless of its technical outcomes — corrodes the legitimacy of governance and deepens the disengagement that already plagues the republic.

“An election that citizens do not trust is not a democracy functioning. It is a democracy performing.”

What Went Wrong in 2023

The 2023 presidential election was, by INEC’s own design, supposed to be its most transparent ever. The Bimodal Voter Accreditation System (BVAS) was introduced to prevent the manipulation of voter accreditation.[²] The INEC Result Viewing Portal (IReV) was launched with the promise of real-time upload of polling unit results[²] — a measure designed to make result falsification visible and traceable.

Both innovations failed, or were failed, at critical moments. The IReV portal experienced what INEC described as technical difficulties during the critical early hours of result transmission. Many results appeared hours or days late. Discrepancies between polling unit results and collated figures were documented by domestic and international observers. INEC’s own officials were arraigned in some states for alleged electoral malpractice. Verified[³]

The commission, under Chairman Prof. Mahmood Yakubu, maintained that the election was credible. The courts agreed, in the main. But credibility is not only a legal determination — it is a social one. And socially, the 2023 election left millions of Nigerians unconvinced.

The Road to 2027

What must change before 2027 to restore citizen confidence? Three things, at minimum: transparent technology, independent oversight, and civic preparedness.

On technology: INEC must not only deploy electronic systems but must demonstrate, through pre-election public testing and open-source verification, that those systems are tamper-resistant and reliably connected. The failure of IReV in 2023 cannot be excused a second time.

On oversight: The composition of INEC’s leadership and its appointment process must be revisited. An electoral commission whose chair is appointed solely by the executive, without meaningful legislative or civil society oversight, cannot credibly claim independence. Electoral reform legislation that restructures this dynamic is an urgent democratic necessity.

On civic preparedness: NITIZEN exists, in part, to address this third dimension. A citizenry that understands the electoral process — that knows how to verify voter registration, identify polling units, document irregularities, and report violations — is a citizenry that is harder to disenfranchise. Electoral literacy is not a soft complement to structural reform. It is a structural reform in itself.

The 2027 elections are Nigeria’s next defining democratic moment. Whether they represent progress or regression will depend significantly on what INEC, the political class, and citizens do between now and then. NITIZEN will be watching — and equipping Nigerians to watch too.

Buhari Promised Security.
Here Is the Record.

NITIZEN EDITORIAL · MAY 2026 · 10 MIN READ

In 2015, Muhammadu Buhari campaigned on a single, clarion promise above all others: security. The Goodluck Jonathan administration, his campaign argued, had failed Nigerians in the most fundamental duty of the state — the protection of lives and territory. Boko Haram had seized swathes of the northeast. Kidnappings had become routine. Farmers and herders were clashing across the Middle Belt in conflicts that left thousands dead. Nigeria, the argument went, needed a general — a man of iron discipline who understood security from the inside.

Nigerians, millions of them, believed this argument. Buhari won decisively. He served two terms — 2015 to 2023. What follows is not an opinion. It is a record.

The Promises, Measured

The PromiseThe RecordVerdict
"We will defeat Boko Haram technically within months" Boko Haram and ISWAP remained operationally active at end of tenure; thousands of military personnel killed; large areas of the northeast remained under threat ⚠ Broken
"Kidnapping will be brought under control" School kidnappings escalated — Chibok (2014, pre-Buhari but unresolved), Kankara (2020), Kagara (2021), Tegina (2021). Bandits openly demanded and received ransoms ⚠ Broken
"Farmer-herder conflicts will be resolved" Deaths from farmer-herder clashes exceeded 7,000 between 2016 and 2022 by multiple estimates. No structured resolution framework was implemented ⚠ Broken
"Banditry will be crushed" Banditry expanded from northwestern states to become a national security crisis. The government at points engaged bandits in dialogue rather than military prosecution ⚠ Broken
"Security forces will be properly equipped and motivated" Partial improvement in equipment; families of fallen troops protested unpaid death benefits; reports of soldiers abandoning positions due to morale failures ◑ Partial
"The military will not interfere in political processes" No military coup or direct military interference in civilian governance occurred during the Buhari years ✓ Kept

The Counting of the Dead

Statistics are cold. Behind each number is a name, a family, a community shattered. But numbers are what governance must ultimately answer to — because governance is a promise made at scale. The Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project (ACLED) documented tens of thousands of conflict-related deaths in Nigeria during the Buhari years. The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre estimated that Nigeria had over 3.6 million internally displaced persons by 2022 — one of the largest displaced populations in Africa.

These figures do not prove that a different administration would have performed better. Security challenges of this complexity are not solved by political will alone. What they do prove is this: the promise was made, the promise was specific, and the promise was not kept. In a functioning democracy, that matters. Citizens deserve to know.

"The purpose of Critika is not vengeance against past administrations. It is the cultivation of a civic reflex — the habit of measuring governance against its own words, and demanding answers for the distance between them."

Why This Matters for 2027

We review the Buhari security record not to relitigate the past but to equip Nigerians for the future. Every candidate who contests in 2027 will make promises — on security, on the economy, on electricity, on education, on corruption. Nigerian citizens who have learned to hold past promises against present performance will be better equipped to evaluate those new promises critically rather than emotionally.

That is the work of Critika. Not rage — rigour. Not complaint — evidence. Not silence — art, data, and the refusal to forget what was said. Every government makes promises. Every promise leaves a record. And every citizen has both the right and the responsibility to read that record clearly.

This is civic accountability. This is Critika.
